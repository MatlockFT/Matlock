*From the Gracie family's rivalry with Japanese professional wrestling to the arrival of the UFC, PRIDE understood how to give its audience someone to cheer for, someone to root against and a reason to care about the outcome.*

## The Gracies and the Birth of PRIDE

In December 1994, Yoji Anjo arrived at Rickson Gracie's academy in California accompanied by members of the Japanese press. Anjo represented UWF International, a professional wrestling promotion whose realistic, submission-heavy style blurred the distinction between wrestling and legitimate competition. His intention was to challenge Gracie, whose family's success in NHB (no holds barred) fighting had become an embarrassment for parts of Japan's wrestling establishment. The challenge ended with Anjo receiving a beating, providing another reminder that professional wrestling's claims of fighting superiority didn't always hold up outside its own environment.

Three years later, the rivalry became the foundation of [PRIDE Fighting Championships](https://www.sherdog.com/organizations/Pride-Fighting-Championships-3). Its inaugural event, held at the Tokyo Dome on October 11, 1997, was built around a meeting between [Nobuhiko Takada](https://www.sherdog.com/fighter/Nobuhiko-Takada-293), UWFi's biggest star, and [Rickson Gracie](https://www.sherdog.com/fighter/Rickson-Gracie-292), who had won Japan's Vale Tudo tournaments in 1994 and 1995. Takada was the obvious representative of Japanese professional wrestling, although there was little in his legitimate fighting experience to suggest he would be competitive with Rickson. At [PRIDE 1](https://www.sherdog.com/events/Pride-FC-Pride-1-46), Gracie took him down, secured mount and finished him with an armbar at 4:47 of the opening round. Their rematch the following year ended in another submission victory for Gracie.



A retrospective published in the Japanese magazine *Kamipro* examined PRIDE's early history through the familiar language of professional wrestling, particularly its use of babyfaces and heels. Takada represented the home side, while the Gracies were the foreign invaders whose dominance Japanese fans wanted to see challenged. The promotion could draw on years of professional wrestling history to establish that rivalry, even if the fighters themselves had no obligation to follow the expected storyline. The Gracies kept winning, Takada had failed twice, and Japanese wrestling fans were still looking for someone who could beat them.

## The Rise of Kazushi Sakuraba

Only two months after the first Takada–Gracie fight, [Kazushi Sakuraba](https://www.sherdog.com/fighter/Kazushi-Sakuraba-84) entered the heavyweight tournament at [UFC Japan](https://www.sherdog.com/events/UFC-155-Ultimate-Japan-1-24). His opening bout against Marcus Silveira, a much larger Brazilian representing Carlson Gracie's team, ended controversially when referee John McCarthy mistakenly believed Sakuraba had been knocked out. The result was changed to a no-contest, and following an injury to another tournament participant, Sakuraba received a rematch with Silveira later that evening. He won by armbar and afterward declared that professional wrestlers really were strong.

Sakuraba's success soon made him one of PRIDE's main attractions, and in November 1999 he faced [Royler Gracie at PRIDE 8](https://www.sherdog.com/events/Pride-FC-Pride-8-53). Negotiations over the rules became part of the buildup, with the Gracie camp securing two 15-minute rounds, no judges and restrictions on the referee's ability to stop the contest. Sakuraba eventually trapped Royler in a kimura late in the second round, prompting the referee to intervene despite Royler refusing to submit. The stoppage was controversial, especially given the agreed conditions, but it did little to discourage Japanese fans who had finally seen a professional wrestler defeat a member of the Gracie family.

The rivalry reached its peak on May 1, 2000, when Sakuraba met [Royce Gracie at the PRIDE Grand Prix Finals](https://www.sherdog.com/events/Pride-FC-Pride-Grand-Prix-2000-Finals-55). Royce's camp again demanded special rules, including unlimited 15-minute rounds and no judges. PRIDE publicized those negotiations, which the *Kamipro* writer later identified as an important part of the fight's appeal. Royce's insistence on special treatment made him the heel, while Sakuraba's willingness to accept the conditions further endeared him to Japanese audiences. After six rounds and 90 minutes of fighting, Royce's corner finally stopped the contest. Sakuraba then returned to face the much larger Igor Vovchanchyn later that evening, withdrawing after the opening round.

Sakuraba defeated Renzo and Ryan Gracie before the end of 2000, although a meeting with Rickson never materialized. His popularity had grown beyond the novelty of a professional wrestler succeeding in MMA. He had become the central figure in a rivalry that PRIDE could revisit with every new Gracie opponent, drawing on the history of previous encounters to build anticipation for the next. The promotion's storytelling was particularly effective because audiences already understood what was at stake, even when the participants weren't competing for a championship.

## When the Villains Became Heroes

Sakuraba's run against the Gracies came to an abrupt interruption on March 25, 2001, when he faced [Wanderlei Silva](https://www.sherdog.com/fighter/Wanderlei-Silva-209) at [PRIDE 13](https://www.sherdog.com/events/Pride-13-Collision-Course-84). The Brazilian, fighting out of the notoriously aggressive Chute Boxe academy, overwhelmed him in just 98 seconds. Silva defeated Sakuraba again that November before knocking him out in their third meeting at [PRIDE Total Elimination 2003](https://www.sherdog.com/events/Pride-FC-Total-Elimination-2003-1693). For an audience that had spent years celebrating Sakuraba's victories, Silva was an easy fighter to dislike.

PRIDE's promotional videos reinforced that reputation, portraying Silva and Chute Boxe as a dangerous presence that threatened Japan's established favorites. However, the longer Silva remained in PRIDE, the more difficult it became to regard him simply as the villain. He defeated Kiyoshi Tamura, Hiromitsu Kanehara, Hidehiko Yoshida and Yuki Kondo, while establishing himself as one of the promotion's most dependable attractions. Japanese fans eventually embraced him, even though he was the same intimidating fighter who had beaten Sakuraba three times. In the *Kamipro* retrospective, Silva's popularity was presented as an example of a heel turning babyface through his accomplishments in the ring.

[Mirko Cro Cop](https://www.sherdog.com/fighter/Mirko-Filipovic-2326) experienced something similar. He arrived from the rival K-1 kickboxing scene, making his PRIDE debut against Takada in November 2001 under special rules that produced a draw. His powerful left kick and increasingly impressive victories soon made him one of the organization's biggest attractions. By the time he challenged [Fedor Emelianenko](https://www.sherdog.com/fighter/Fedor-Emelianenko-1500) at [PRIDE Final Conflict 2005](https://www.sherdog.com/events/Pride-FC-Final-Conflict-2005-3184), Cro Cop had spent years pursuing the heavyweight champion, including knocking out Fedor's brother Aleksander along the way. Their long-awaited meeting ended in a unanimous decision for Fedor, but Cro Cop had already earned his place among PRIDE's most popular fighters.

The transformation of Silva and Cro Cop was particularly interesting because it changed the definition of whom Japanese audiences considered their own. PRIDE had begun with Takada defending professional wrestling against the Gracies, but nationality had become less important than a fighter's association with the promotion. Silva and Cro Cop had arrived as outsiders and eventually became representatives of PRIDE itself.

## PRIDE Against K-1

That loyalty extended to the promotion's rivalry with K-1. The two organizations had previously cooperated, most notably at the enormous Dynamite!! event in August 2002, but their relationship deteriorated as they competed for fighters, television audiences and control of the Japanese combat sports market. Beginning in 2003, PRIDE staged its own New Year's Eve *Otoko Matsuri* events opposite K-1's *Dynamite!!*, making December 31 an annual contest for television ratings and mainstream attention.

K-1 increasingly pursued celebrity attractions involving figures such as Bob Sapp, former sumo grand champion Akebono and Bobby Ologun, while PRIDE promoted its leading matchups around established sporting rivalries. PRIDE certainly wasn't above putting on spectacles of its own, but the distinction was useful to its promoters. As *Kamipro* observed, K-1 provided PRIDE with an organizational heel, giving supporters a rival to oppose even when their favorite Japanese fighters weren't involved. The audience could now cheer for Silva, Cro Cop or any other established PRIDE attraction against an outsider.

Much of that attachment was also cultivated through the promotion's presentation. The elaborate entrances, fighter introductions and promotional films helped establish the history behind an upcoming contest, often revisiting past defeats or rivalries rather than simply showing a collection of highlights. Filmmaker [Daisuke Sato](https://knockdownnews.com/2021/12/27/daisuke-sato-the-storyteller-of-jmma/) became particularly influential in shaping that presentation. His videos gave audiences a sense of where a fighter had come from and why a particular opponent mattered, which proved especially useful when PRIDE was introducing unfamiliar international competitors.

## The UFC Arrives

By 2005, the UFC was enjoying renewed popularity in the United States following the success of *The Ultimate Fighter*. The competition between the organizations attracted considerable attention from fans who had spent years debating which promotion possessed the better fighters. Chuck Liddell had already competed in PRIDE's 2003 Middleweight Grand Prix, losing to Quinton Jackson in the semifinals, and Wanderlei Silva appeared in the UFC Octagon in July 2006 to promote a prospective fight with Liddell. Negotiations failed to produce the matchup that year, but the possibility of bringing the two rosters together remained a major attraction.

Dana White's confrontational personality made him an obvious candidate to play the antagonist in another PRIDE rivalry. On March 27, 2007, following the [announcement that the UFC's owners were purchasing PRIDE](https://www.sherdog.com/news/articles/PRIDE-to-Fans-We146re-Not-Going-Anywhere-7093), White and Lorenzo Fertitta appeared at a press conference in Tokyo to discuss the promotion's future. The plan involved operating the companies separately while arranging occasional crossover fights between their leading athletes. White embraced the competitive relationship, telling the Japanese audience that the UFC was coming to defeat PRIDE. For supporters accustomed to the promotion's history of outside challengers, it was a familiar proposition.

The promised rivalry never developed. PRIDE held its [final event on April 8, 2007](https://www.sherdog.com/events/Pride-34-Kamikaze-4725), and its Japanese office closed that October. Although former PRIDE fighters would eventually compete against UFC stars, those matchups took place under the UFC banner rather than as contests between two operating organizations. PRIDE's financial difficulties and the loss of its Fuji Television relationship had already threatened its survival before the acquisition, but the disappearance of the promotion was still a considerable loss for its supporters.

The *Kamipro* writer offered an interesting interpretation of White's role in the final chapter. Unlike the Gracies, Silva or K-1, the UFC never became an opponent that PRIDE could challenge through competition. White had the personality of a wrestling heel, but the organization's disappearance meant there was no longer a rivalry for audiences to follow. The magazine argued that he wasn't really a heel at all, but an enemy who had taken away the place where those stories happened.

## The Lost Art of the Heel

Nearly twenty years later, it's difficult not to notice how differently PRIDE approached the promotion of its fighters. The UFC has produced tremendous rivalries of its own, including Anderson Silva and Chael Sonnen, Jon Jones and Daniel Cormier, and Conor McGregor and Khabib Nurmagomedov. Yet much of its modern promotion seems centered on individual personalities, press conference exchanges and whatever attraction happens to be headlining the next event. There is less of the continuing history that PRIDE regularly brought into its presentation.

Of course, PRIDE benefited from a Japanese audience already familiar with professional wrestling's storytelling traditions, and some of its rivalries were deliberately manufactured. The promotion also had its share of questionable matchmaking and spectacles that had little to do with sporting merit. Still, it recognized the value of allowing past results to influence the audience's relationship with a fighter. Sakuraba's victories over the Gracies changed his career, while his defeats to Silva eventually helped create another major attraction. PRIDE could draw on those experiences for years, even when the fighters involved had moved on to different opponents.

That's probably what I find most absent from the modern UFC. There are still plenty of compelling fighters and interesting rivalries, but the organization doesn't consistently give those stories the same attention or sense of continuity. PRIDE was especially good at reminding its audience why a fight mattered beyond the immediate rankings or championship picture, and it understood that a fighter's reputation could evolve considerably over time.

The *Kamipro* writer's distinction between a heel and an enemy is an appropriate place to leave that history. PRIDE spent a decade giving its audience opponents to root against, and some of those opponents eventually became its most popular fighters. By 2007, the UFC appeared ready to become its biggest rival, with the prospect of settling years of arguments through actual competition. Instead, the promotion disappeared before that rivalry could properly begin.
