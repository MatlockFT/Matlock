**Alexander Volkanovski** is still one of the most complete fighters in MMA. He builds everything off feints, stance changes and angle shifts, mixes his boxing with hard body and leg work, and has the wrestling and cardio to keep changing the fight over five rounds. He has now beaten Diego Lopes twice since the back-to-back knockout losses to Islam Makhachev and Ilia Topuria.

**Movsar Evloev** is an undefeated chain wrestler who is very good at turning one failed shot into the next position. He pressures opponents toward the fence, keeps working through scrambles and has become much more comfortable boxing his way into those exchanges. He is 10-0 in the UFC and does not need a finish to control long stretches of a fight.

**Lone'er Kavanagh** is a fast, technical kickboxer who likes to change stance, create angles and counter as opponents step into range. The win over Brandon Moreno showed he can stay composed and effective over five rounds, while the Royval loss was another reminder that the scramble and submission layer is still dangerous territory for him.

**Ramazan Temirov** is a compact pressure striker with real first-round power. He throws hard in combination, closes distance quickly and has started his UFC run 3-0, most recently knocking out Steve Erceg in the first round. The 100% takedown defense looks great, but it is still a pretty small UFC sample.

**Alexander Volkov** is a rangy, high-volume heavyweight who uses his jab, front kicks and body attacks better than most big men. His clinch work and top game have become much more useful as he has gotten older, and he has put together a strong run by making opponents deal with his size for the full fight.

**Rizvan Kuniev** is a strong, compact heavyweight who pressures well, works the body and is difficult to wrestle cleanly. He lost a close split decision to Curtis Blaydes in his UFC debut, then came back with a decision over Jailton Almeida and a third-round stoppage of Tyrell Fortune.

**Aaron Pico** is an orthodox fighter with a boxing-heavy offense and a strong wrestling background. He throws hard punches to the head and body and can mix in takedowns when he chooses to wrestle. He was knocked out by Lerone Murphy in his UFC debut but bounced back with a decision win over Patricio Pitbull, making better use of his wrestling throughout the fight.

**Losene Keita** is an orthodox striker who uses his footwork to close distance, find openings and land hard counters. He lost a close split decision to Nathaniel Wood in his UFC debut before knocking out Muhammad Naimov in the first round. His takedown defense has held up in his first two UFC fights, but he hasn't faced much sustained wrestling pressure at this level yet.

Keita is super fun to watch. He likes to pressure, loves to counter. I'd say he has pretty good eyes and solid reactive speed.

**Azamat Murzakanov** is a compact southpaw with a lot of power and a good feel for countering as opponents enter. He attacks the body well and was 6-0 in the UFC before Paulo Costa handed him his first career loss. Even at 37, his combination of power and takedown defense makes him a difficult matchup.

**Dominick Reyes** is a long southpaw power striker who does his best work with the straight left and intercepting counters. He has rebuilt himself after the ugly losing streak and has won four of his last five, although the Carlos Ulberg loss showed he can still get caught clean when the pocket gets chaotic.

I agree with the odds for this fight. Earlier in his career, I think Reyes would have smashed someone like Azamat with his speed, timing, and movement. He put together a phenomenal game plan against Jon Jones and executed it very well. He went through some tough fights and a notorious losing streak, but he has put together a respectable comeback despite his KO loss to Ulberg, who is now the champion.

Although Reyes has taken some clean shots in his last several fights, I'd still say his chin is suspect. I genuinely think a clean shot from Azamat Murzakanov could put him out. I think Dominick's best path to victory is to take a few pages out of Paulo Costa's playbook: hammer the kicks and keep moving. Reyes already has a pretty decent kicking game. His entire fight with Johnny Walker, for example, was practically a kicking match. I also favor Reyes to have the better conditioning. On top of that, he has simply faced better opposition. What's even more hilarious is that Reyes is a year younger than Azamat.

Reyes is definitely a live dog here. I could see him winning by decision or getting a stoppage in the later rounds.

Another observation is that this is a southpaw vs. southpaw matchup. For what it's worth, Dustin Jacoby did some of his best work against Azamat when he switched to southpaw. Could that benefit Reyes, who rarely switches stances and fights primarily from southpaw?

Paulo Costa had so much success with his kicks because of the open-stance matchup. He hammered Azamat with well-timed leg kicks as Murzakanov loaded his weight onto his lead leg to explode, knocking him off balance. Costa then attacked the body before going all-in on head kicks in the third round.

With all due respect to Reyes, I don't think his timing is as good as Costa's. I also don't think he kicks as hard, or at least he hasn't shown that same kind of power. On top of that, he isn't primarily an orthodox striker.

That leaves me wondering whether Reyes can maintain his distance for three rounds without getting backed against the cage or caught by one of Azamat's bombs. Murzakanov does well against taller fighters who like to kick and fight at range, as we saw against Rakic. To be fair, Rakic made some of the lowest-IQ decisions imaginable by repeatedly going for single legs against Azamat when he was doing perfectly fine at range.

Ribeiro was also having success at range, but Murzakanov's ability to bounce on the balls of his feet, fire lightning-fast hands, and explode into his attacks eventually put him on his ass. Reyes has more to worry about than just the overhand. Azamat can attack the body and head with his jab, work in uppercuts, and land knees. He is especially dangerous in the first round.

I think Reyes has a real chance to win, but I can't trust his chin, and I think his reactions have slowed. Also, he has bad striking defense in general. Hands down, chin up type of stuff. 

But this is a fight he *should* win. Then again, we thought that about Blachowicz, Prochazka, Spann... he was favored in all those fights. 

That said, I think it's a close fight. If I have to weigh Azamat's questionable cardio against Reyes' questionable chin, I'm taking Azamat's cardio. Not because his conditioning is horrendous, but because he noticeably slows down midway through the second round. He's still dangerous and explosive, but if the fight reaches that point, things should become a hell of a lot less dangerous for Reyes.

I just don't think we'll see that happen. Azamat knocks out Dominick Reyes with a jab and the right hand follow up will just send him to the land of wind and ghosts before he hits the mat.

**Nikita Krylov** is a well-rounded veteran who can hurt opponents on the feet, wrestle, and threaten submissions when fights get messy. He's particularly dangerous in scrambles, where he can transition quickly between striking and grappling. The problem is that his defense can get sloppy, and he has a habit of getting caught when exchanges turn into brawls. He's experienced enough to compete with just about anyone in the division, but he's also shown that he can be hurt and finished.

**Abdul Rakhman Yakhyaev** has made an impressive start in the UFC, finishing all three of his opponents in the first round. He's shown finishing ability with both his striking and grappling, including two submission wins and a knockout. The biggest question is how he handles a fight that doesn't go his way early. He hasn't had to show much of his game beyond those opening exchanges, so it's difficult to judge his cardio, adjustments, or ability to work through adversity. Krylov represents a significant step up in experience, and Yakhyaev will have to prove he can maintain his effectiveness when the fight gets competitive.

Yakhyaev has done a great job of overwhelming opponents with his speed and reaction time. Opponents have to be extremely careful about throwing lazy jabs or backing straight up because he'll slip the shot and come over the top. He does it consistently. He'll take the center, throw a big kick, wait for the response, slip the counter, and come back with a flurry of strikes.

The question is how well that translates against more experienced, well-rounded opposition. I still have serious questions about his wrestling against a fighter who can match him in that department. Luckily for Yakhyaev, high-level wrestlers are few and far between at light heavyweight. His lone amateur loss came against an opponent who outwrestled him, although that was seven years ago. We haven't seen enough against formidable opposition in that area to know how much he's improved.

There have been some interesting moments in his recent footage, too. Ribeiro landed a solid knee to the body as Yakhyaev blitzed into range, and he did well to avoid another rush and counter with a 1-2 while moving backward. . Yakhyaev appeared to panic and dive for an ankle on one takedown attempt.

It's kind of wild to see Krylov is only 34 because it genuinely feels like he should be older. I've been watching this dude since the beginning. 😭 He's had a rough stretch, getting stopped by Reyes and Guskov before bouncing back with a buzzer-beater KO over Modestas Bukauskas, only to get stopped by Whittaker in his most recent fight. I'll give him credit for absorbing Bukauskas's shots, figuring him out, and finding the finish with practically no time left. He also made it into the third round against Whittaker before a jaw injury forced the stoppage. So I wouldn't say his chin is completely shot, but there are legitimate concerns about his decline.

The question is whether he's declined enough to lose to someone as inexperienced as Yakhyaev.

Stylistically, these two have some similarities. Krylov throws knees, kicks, and straight punches. He looks to slip and counter, and he'll finish combinations with head kicks. The problem is how he handles pressure and movement going backward. Krylov often looks completely exposed while retreating in straight lines. He'll throw counters when his back is against the fence, but he hasn't consistently shown that he can defend himself effectively while moving backward in open space.

Those knees are worth watching, too. Krylov will throw them while standing on one leg, leaving himself vulnerable to counters. And perhaps most importantly, he gets countered off his own forward blitzes pretty often. That feeds directly into Yakhyaev's game. If Krylov comes charging forward, he could give Yakhyaev exactly the openings he wants.

This fight might just be a car crash, and I can absolutely see a scenario where Krylov survives the initial onslaught. Will Yakhyaev's explosiveness and speed be enough to get him out of there in the first round? Can he drop Krylov, pounce on him, and land enough ground-and-pound to force the referee to step in?

The odds suggest Yakhyaev is practically a lock. I agree that if these two crash into each other, Yakhyaev is the more likely man to walk away with the win, but I'm not buying him at -500. I don't trust him that much. There are still levels to this game, even if Krylov hasn't looked great lately.

If Krylov survives the first round, things could get interesting. He might settle in, make some adjustments, and maybe, just maybe, win the final two rounds. That's a real scenario worth considering when you're looking at a price this steep. I also would not at all be surprised if Krylov can set something up and sit Yahkyaev down.

As for the ground game, I really don't know what to make of it. I'd expect Krylov to have enough experience to defend some takedowns or at least keep himself safe on the bottom, but experience doesn't automatically translate to effective defense. I'm not sure how much success he'll have offensively, either. Yakhyaev's explosiveness and ability to capitalize on scrambles could make things difficult.

I'd genuinely like to pick Krylov here because I'm skeptical of Yakhyaev, but I can't quite get there. I think Krylov will crash forward, and that could be exactly what Yakhyaev needs to land the counter that puts him away.

Still, I'm not comfortable laying -500 on an inexperienced fighter with unanswered questions against a veteran who, for all his flaws, has been in there with some of the best in the sport. I'm picking Yakhyaev, but I'm not convinced the price reflects the actual risk.

Believe me, I'll be watching this fight closely, with my eye locked on those live odds. If Krylov survives the first round and starts finding his footing, I want to see what the market does.

Cam can look fairly flat-footed on the mat, and he doesn't always use his feet actively to adjust his position or maintain control. Against certain opponents, that has cost him positions he's worked hard to earn. It's not necessarily a dealbreaker, but it's another area where I think Magomedov could have an advantage.

I'm having a tough time imagining how this fight plays out because these guys are so similar in size. If Cam can keep things simple with meat-and-potatoes striking and movement, I think that'll get him pretty far. Teeps, straight punches, and constantly keeping that jab in his opponent's face. He's already very good at that.

What gives me pause is his fight with Torrez Finney. I thought Cam was clearly the better striker, but he couldn't get Torrez off him. It's tough to draw any definitive conclusions from a weird fight like that, but it does make me wonder whether Magomedov can find similar success with pressure and control. If this fight makes it to the third round, that'll be an interesting dynamic to watch.

I think Cam Rowston has Neil Magny-type potential but at middleweight, however, I'm not ready to trust him at this level yet. There's enough to like in his striking, but I haven't seen enough to know how he'll handle opponents who can take away his space and force him into uncomfortable positions. Based on what I've seen outside the UFC, on Contender Series, and in his UFC matchups so far, there are still too many unanswered questions for me to make a confident pick. Cam Rowston is a good pick simply because he's not 36 and he isn't a gasser like Magomedov. Skill for skill? I'll go with Abus Magomedov.

Aliev does everything pretty well, but nothing I've seen has stood out as particularly great. He's scrappy and dynamic enough offensively on the feet, with herky-jerky movement and feints from the outside that are reminiscent of Khabib Nurmagomedov. His striking defense leaves a lot to be desired, though, and he gets hit with his hands down quite frequently, regardless of what the striking stats say. I've also noticed he's not particularly good at blocking body kicks. There are openings here.

He gets takedowns, but some of his shots are pretty ugly. None of his competition has been able to consistently thwart his attempts, although opponents have managed to stop him at times and get back to their feet from bottom. It's not as though Aliev has a devastating ground game, but what he does has been effective. He's also put himself in triangle attempts in a couple of different fights. He hasn't faced anything he couldn't escape or come particularly close to being submitted by, but it's still worth noting.

His conditioning is pretty good, although I have questions about how he'll hold up in a third round against someone who can maintain a high pace and pressure. He sometimes looks a little winded and slow, but when he's fighting at his own pace and tempo, which he usually manages to do, he can get through fights without much trouble.

Grant Dawson loves forward pressure and likes to make fights ugly, ultimately looking to end up on top, do damage, or advance position to take the back. His standup game, in my opinion, is primarily built around kicks, and some of them are downright ugly. There are times when he throws nice ones, but more often than not, they're extremely telegraphed, with plenty of windup. A fighter with sharper reads can stay out of range or counter them. The same goes for his hands. He throws big punches from well outside punching range and also likes to throw spinning shit, both punches and kicks. His best attributes are his forward pressure, conditioning, and well-rounded grappling and wrestling.

This is solid matchmaking because Dawson and Aliev have similar strengths. I think Dawson is the better overall grappler and could find submissions against him. Their wrestling is more competitive, and I'm particularly interested in seeing how they match up against the cage. At range, they're both pretty bad, but I'd give Aliev the edge because there's a little more form to his striking. At the very least, it's not quite as messy, although it's still ugly, if that makes sense. Both also tend to get drawn into slugfests in the pocket.

When I think about their striking at range, I don't see much of an advantage for either guy. Dawson might have a slight edge through activity and landing nonsense shots to the arms, but he also has opportunities to land body kicks if he can set them up. Aliev has been targeted there before. At close range, I favor Aliev to land the better punches, although I'm not convinced they'll be fight-ending shots. They'll likely be cleaner and do more meaningful damage. Dawson's best opportunities up close could come from collar-tie uppercuts and knees to the body as Aliev comes forward. Aliev hunches over quite a lot, especially when he's winging punches to close the distance, so I see a big opportunity for Dawson to land knees.

More interesting are their takedown attempts from range, something both fighters do regularly. They dive for single legs and knee picks, and I think both can defend each other's takedowns in open space. The better question is who has the superior timing and setups. I'd argue it's nearly even here, and I find it difficult to make a confident call before fight night.

Against the cage, it's a trickier matchup to assess, but I do think Aliev chains things together a little better along the fence. The difference is probably nominal. I could see them battling for position and ultimately nullifying each other.

However, if either man gets the other down, I think Dawson's grappling could be the difference-maker. He's more opportunistic with submissions, and Aliev has shown more susceptibility to them. Dawson has fourteen submission victories, after all.

The other side of that equation is Dawson failing to secure anything and spending too much time hunting for submissions at the expense of position. That's essentially what happened in Aliev's fights against Soleicki and Shem Rock. Both found good submission opportunities but couldn't capitalize on them. Rock also wasn't effective enough on the feet, and by the time the third round arrived, he was making terrible decisions trying to find a way to win. Aliev simply needed to secure top position again to take the decision. That same scenario could play out here. The difference is that Dawson is much more of a dawg and will bring the fight to Aliev with relentless pressure and some of the ugliest striking you'll see.

Personally, I find this an incredibly tricky fight to pick. I can make a convincing argument for either guy. Dawson's entire game should be coming together at this stage of his career, with his mentality, training, fight IQ, and skill development all maturing. In theory, this is when he should be at his most dangerous. He is, however, fighting in Abu Dhabi, so if this goes to the scorecards in a relatively close fight, it's possible the judges could favor one fighter over the other, if you believe in that sort of thing.

For me, I'll give Aliev a slight lean. He's proven against grapplers that he can survive and work his way out of bad positions. He's the better athlete, has the better standup game, even if only marginally, and is younger with less mileage on him. But don't believe for a second that Grant Dawson can't survive and thrive in the latter half of the second round and into the third, dig deep, and put his veteran experience to work against Aliev.

Dawson by submission or Aliev by decision are both perfectly reasonable picks for me.

Caliari is a very frustrating fighter to watch. She's had a tumultuous relationship with the sport of MMA, to say the least. It seems she's finally gotten her weight issues under control after getting her implants removed, and she's now fighting at strawweight, where she should be. More than that, though, her game hasn't really developed significantly since her pro debut.

There are brief moments in fights where it seems like she has a grip on what she wants to do in the striking, only to ultimately abandon it, throw down, and crash into a clinch. She's got power in her right hand, and her kicks aren't bad either. But she struggles to adapt at range against someone who gets the better of her in the exchanges. Eventually, she'll blitz forward, create a bit of chaos, and force a clinch, hopefully finding something else to work with from there.

Any fighter who can fight off the back foot, counter effectively, and has even a little ability to break from the clinch will give Caliari problems. She's a very emotional fighter, and I'd be willing to bet she's a nightmare to coach. That being said, she seems to have made some minor improvements to her throws and positioning on the mat, though I think that's mostly because she's finally fighting at the appropriate weight class. Iasmin Lucindo is a BJJ player who excels on top and has made considerable strides with her striking over the years, particularly when it comes to landing heavy counters. She also has a fondness for jumping knees and spinning shit. Her footwork remains a work in progress; she's borderline Cat Zingano from the waist down, although there's been noticeable improvement. Lucindo carries some natural pop, which was likely aided by a few of those supplements she popped for.

Her fight IQ is pretty good, all things considered, and I reckon it played a major role in securing the decision over Marina Rodriguez, who couldn't get out of her own way. If you had Rodriguez winning that one, Lucindo sits at 3-2 across her last five outings. She's more than happy to let an opponent march forward and may even prefer it, given the opportunities to crack them with those clubbing counters.

In the clinch, she's capable of tying opponents up and using her strength to maintain control until she can bring the action to the mat. Once there, her BJJ is patient and methodical. She's perfectly content to sit in a position for minutes at a time, but the moment her opponent starts moving underneath her, she'll use that opening to progress toward a more dominant spot, create openings for ground-and-pound, or work toward a submission.

This matchup has a good chance of turning into a slugfest. Neither woman is reluctant to step into the pocket and trade hooks. They've both spent time dealing with those spindly, rangy strikers, so this should offer a more entertaining change of pace.

Lucindo gets the nod from me in the power department, and she appears more conscious of distance and pressure on both sides of the exchange. Should things inevitably turn into a clinch-heavy wrestling match, I give her the edge there, too.

The wildcard is Caliari's scrappiness. She'll chase submissions, hunt for throws, and gladly trade leather in close quarters. Even if the odds are slim, she's certainly capable of finding a clean shot or catching something on the ground. More plausibly, though, she'll try to turn this into a messy, high-output affair, forcing Lucindo to keep working until fatigue and sheer volume give her a path to the scorecards.

Ultimately, I favor the younger, stronger, more composed fighter with the longer reach — who also happens to have been popped for steroids. A KO/TKO wouldn't surprise me, as Lucindo could accumulate enough damage to put Caliari away. Still, this is MMA at 115 pounds, where stoppages can be difficult to predict, so a decision remains the safer pick.
