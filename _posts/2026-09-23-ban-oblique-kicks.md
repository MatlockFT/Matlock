Finally! The MMA Gods have listened to the collective prayers of casual and hardcore fans alike as they have anointed the great [Andy Foster](https://www.tapology.com/fightcenter/fighters/4052-andy-foster) to look into a technique that has shelved and ended the careers of many an MMA fighter. The powerful and deadly technique goes by many names but I, as a die-hard Joe Rogan loyalist, call it the OBLIQUE KICK. 

This controversial kick has been used by many but most notably by Anderson Silva, Jon Jones, and Khalil Rountree. It's no wonder in some circles its called the illegal kick because its impossible to block or counter. In fact, unlike a submission, you cannot even tap to it. Its truly amazing we've made it to 2026 without it being banned yet. 

If you haven't felt a shiver up your spine at merely reading the words OBLIQUE KICK then that means you are unfamiliar with the devastating move. Worry not, for there is a video explaining it in perfect detail by the one who arguably made it most famous and used it most effectively. The one, the only Jonny Bones Jones.

Five years later, the issue has returned to California’s athletic regulators. Following UFC 331, California State Athletic Commission executive director Andy Foster called for discussion of making lateral knee kicks a foul. The renewed attention followed Brunno Ferreira’s use of knee-directed kicks against Edmen Shahbazyan, who complained to the referee during their September 19 fight. The strikes were legal. Shahbazyan won by unanimous decision. [Sherdog’s report](https://www.sherdog.com/news/news/Controversial-MMA-technique-could-be-banned-following-UFC-331-202885/amp), [UFC’s result](https://www.ufc.com/video/160166).

The dispute has a much longer history than either fight.

In ordinary MMA usage, an **oblique kick** is a thrusting kick delivered with the sole or heel toward the opponent’s leg, usually at or just above the knee. Its path differs from the swinging motion of a conventional round kick. It can interrupt forward movement, upset an opponent’s stance and keep a puncher outside punching range. Jon Jones and coach Mike Winkeljohn demonstrated those applications in a 2015 instructional video. [Jones and Winkeljohn’s demonstration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6Lpf1vwr658).

The terminology is less consistent than the public debate suggests. “Oblique kick,” “knee stomp,” “push kick to the knee,” “low-line kick” and “low side kick” often appear interchangeably in MMA coverage. A UFC profile of Winkeljohn also used “shovel kick.” Technically, however, a low side kick and a front-facing oblique kick can involve different body positions and deliveries. They become grouped together because they attack the same general target. [UFC’s Winkeljohn profile](https://us.ufcespanol.com/news/winkeljohns-fight-wrong-right), [Jack Slack’s technical analysis](https://www.fightprimer.com/articles/2019/7/4/ktk).

That distinction matters if regulators write a prohibition. Banning a loosely defined name is different from banning a specified action against the knee.

The technique also predates modern MMA by decades. French savate offers a close technical relative in the **chassé bas**, a low thrusting kick. The French federation’s technical manual distinguishes frontal and lateral chassés and describes their straight, piston-like delivery. Savate itself developed in nineteenth-century France; the federation dates Michel Casseux’s Paris school to 1820 and Charles Lecour’s combination of savate with English boxing to 1838. Those dates establish the older tradition, rather than an exact birthday for the modern MMA kick. [Savate technical manual](https://www.ffsavate.com/content/uploads/2023/05/CAHIER-3-Pedagogie-MAJ-01-2014.pdf), [French federation history](https://www.ffsavate.com/federation/presentation/notre-histoire/).

Bruce Lee’s work supplies another clear antecedent. His published instruction describes attacks to the shin and knee and the use of a **stop kick** to intercept an advancing opponent. *Bruce Lee’s Fighting Method: Advanced Techniques*, published in 1977, includes a section on the leading shin and knee kick. “Stop kick” describes the intercepting function; it does not identify one exclusively knee-directed technique. [Book record and contents](https://books.google.com/books/about/Bruce_Lee_s_Fighting_Method_Advanced_Tec.html?id=5IxGE4K8Gp0C), [Publisher’s illustrated excerpt](https://www.tuttlepublishing.com/content/docs/23B_Trade_Catalog.pdf).

For Jones specifically, Winkeljohn provides a documented coaching connection. In his UFC interview, Winkeljohn discussed bringing techniques from his Kenpo background into MMA, with Jones’s oblique kicks offered as a prominent example. The evidence supports several related martial-arts traditions, rather than a confidently traceable chain from one inventor to every modern practitioner. [UFC’s interview](https://us.ufcespanol.com/news/winkeljohns-fight-wrong-right).

Establishing the technique’s first appearance in MMA is harder. Older fights were not consistently described using today’s vocabulary, and low push kicks, side kicks and knee attacks are frequently conflated.

An early documented example of the broader family appears at **UFC 3 in 1994**, when Keith Hackney used low side kicks against Emmanuel Yarborough. It is a useful historical reference, although it should not be presented as the proven first oblique kick in MMA. [Fight retrospective](https://fightersonly.com/article/ext/73220/Classic%20Fights/1). Low side kicks to the knee appeared in that bout. [FOX Sports retrospective](https://www.foxsports.com/stories/ufc/top-20-wtf-moments-in-ufc-history).

A firmer landmark for the modern rules controversy is **Anderson Silva against Thales Leites at UFC 97 in April 2009**. Their fight prompted an Association of Boxing Commissions committee to review linear strikes to the knee joint. Its conclusion was explicit: “this technique should remain a legal technique.” The regulatory debate was already underway before Jones’s championship reign. [ABC committee report](https://www.abcboxing.com/committee-report-on-unified-rules-for-mma/).

Jones subsequently made those kicks a central part of his public identity. His fights against **Quinton “Rampage” Jackson at UFC 135 in 2011** and **Vitor Belfort at UFC 152 in 2012** helped introduce a much wider audience to the low side kick and oblique kick as systematic tools for controlling distance. They interrupted opponents trying to close the space Jones needed to operate. [Technical analysis of Jones’s use](https://www.fightprimer.com/articles/2019/7/4/ktk).

Jackson became one of the technique’s most prominent critics. In January 2013, he argued that it “should be called the illegal kick,” questioning both its permissibility and the character of fighters who used it. His complaint helped establish the recurring terms of the dispute: an effective, legal weapon to its advocates; an unacceptable threat to an opponent’s livelihood to its critics. [Contemporary report of Jackson’s comments](https://www.mmamania.com/2013/1/22/1536869/rampage-jackson-jon-jones-oblique-kick-illegal-banned-ufc-mma).

The **Robert Whittaker–Yoel Romero fights** added another significant chapter. Whittaker suffered a first-round knee injury at UFC 213 in July 2017 and nevertheless won the interim middleweight championship. He later described a grade-two medial ligament injury and disclosed that he had strained the same knee before the fight. That pre-existing problem is important context when recounting the injury. [UFC’s fight report](https://www.ufc.com/news/robert-whittaker-pounds-his-way-past-yoel-romero-interim-middleweight-strap-ufc-213), [Whittaker’s injury account](https://www.mmafighting.com/2017/7/21/16008974/robert-whittaker-suffered-grade-two-medial-ligament-injury-to-left-knee-at-ufc-213).

Whittaker then used knee-directed kicks extensively in their June 2018 rematch. Stephen Thompson pointed to that reversal when explaining his own objections after fighting Darren Till. [Thompson interview](https://www.bjpenn.com/mma-news/exclusive-interviews/exclusive-should-side-kicks-to-the-knee-be-illegal-in-mma-stephen-thomspon-shares-his-thoughts/).

**Till–Thompson, in May 2018**, became another major reference point. Thompson said a side kick caught the center of his knee, compromised his movement and left him worried about another impact. He reported an MRI-confirmed MCL tear that required at least eight weeks of healing, although no surgery. He called for the strike to be prohibited. [Thompson’s firsthand account](https://www.bjpenn.com/mma-news/exclusive-interviews/exclusive-should-side-kicks-to-the-knee-be-illegal-in-mma-stephen-thomspon-shares-his-thoughts/).

Till defended using the technique, arguing that it belonged within the risks of professional fighting. In July 2020, he was on the receiving end against Whittaker. [Till’s defense](https://mixedmartialarts.com/editorial/darren-till-makes-the-case-for-oblique-kicks3141581f-314a-4b32-ade6-332201dfde67/), [Contemporary play-by-play](https://www.mmamania.com/2020/7/22/21334509/ufc-on-espn-14-live-stream-results-whittaker-vs-till-play-by-play-updates-fight-island-mma). His coach subsequently confirmed a torn MCL. [ESPN’s injury report](https://www.espn.com/mma/story/_/id/29564823/darren-suffers-torn-mcl-cast-six-weeks).

Then came **Rountree–Bukauskas in September 2021**, widely reported as the UFC’s first finish directly attributable to this type of kick. The immediate collapse made the potential consequences unusually visible. It also prompted another announcement that the MMA rules committee would review the technique. [Contemporary review coverage](https://middleeasy.com/mma-news/mma-rules-committee-review-oblique-kick/).

Bukauskas himself resisted blaming Rountree. “It was my job to defend and I didn’t,” he wrote afterward. He later returned to competition and the UFC—a relevant qualification whenever the bout is described as having ended his career. [Bukauskas’s response](https://sports.yahoo.com/modestas-bukauskas-defends-khalil-rountree-184016225.html), [UFC’s account of his return](https://www.ufc.com/news/modestas-bukauskas-ready-to-take-the-next-step-ufc-324).

More recently, **Ian Machado Garry’s December 2024 fight with Shavkat Rakhmonov** entered the discussion. Garry subsequently claimed credit for Rakhmonov’s knee problems and prolonged absence. That claim requires attribution: Daniel Cormier reported immediately after the fight that Rakhmonov had entered it with a serious knee injury. Footage of a kick does not establish responsibility for every subsequent operation or setback. [Garry’s claim](https://www.sherdog.com/news/news/Ian-Garry-Takes-Credit-for-Shavkat-Rakhmonovs-Injury-200012), [Contemporary report of the pre-existing injury](https://www.mmafighting.com/2024/12/9/24316961/daniel-cormier-claims-shavkat-rakhmonov-entered-ufc-310-with-serious-injury).

The arguments remain divided. Critics emphasize the consequences of a direct attack on the knee and the potential for an extended interruption to a fighter’s career. Supporters emphasize its defensive and tactical uses, the possibility of defending it, and the serious injuries already associated with other permitted techniques. Dan Hardy argued against a ban after the Rountree finish; Thompson’s account illustrates the opposing position. [Hardy’s comments](https://www.mmafighting.com/2021/9/8/22662468/dan-hardy-against-banning-oblique-kick-fighters-sign-death-waiver-when-competing-khalil-rountree), [Thompson’s comments](https://www.bjpenn.com/mma-news/exclusive-interviews/exclusive-should-side-kicks-to-the-knee-be-illegal-in-mma-stephen-thomspon-shares-his-thoughts/).

There is already a regulatory precedent in another combat sport. The ABC’s professional kickboxing rules prohibit thrusting or linear kicks directed at the knee joint. Its current professional MMA rules contain no corresponding prohibition. The MMA rule against small-joint manipulation also expressly distinguishes fingers and toes from large joints, including knees. [Professional kickboxing rules](https://www.abcboxing.com/unified-rules-kickboxing/), [August 2026 MMA rules](https://www.abcboxing.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Unified-Rules-of-MMA-8.2026.pdf).

Foster’s renewed discussion therefore raises specific questions: which kicks would a proposed rule cover, how would it distinguish the knee from the surrounding leg, and what evidence would support that boundary? For now, the available reporting establishes a call for discussion. It does not establish a completed ban. [Report of Foster’s proposal](https://www.sherdog.com/news/news/Controversial-MMA-technique-could-be-banned-following-UFC-331-202885/amp).

Before publication, three reporting gaps deserve attention:

- **The “first” claim:** UFC 3 is an early documented example of a related knee-directed side kick. I could not establish the first exact oblique kick in MMA or the UFC.
- **The proposed rule:** Obtain Foster’s actual draft language, intended jurisdictional scope and any meeting or voting date.
- **The injury comparison:** The cases establish that serious injuries occur. They do not establish the injury rate per kick or its relative risk compared with other legal techniques.
