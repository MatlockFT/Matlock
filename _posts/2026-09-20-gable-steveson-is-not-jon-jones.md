Every once in a while, MMA delivers in the most satisfying way possible: the bad guy gets completely decimated.

I'd like to say the decimation was delivered by a good guy, but this sport will teach you not to judge people too quickly.

From what we know so far, Sean Sharaf appears to be one of the good guys. He's a U.S. Marine, talks about wanting to help support his mother in retirement, and is proud of his Palestinian descent. 

He is, however, in substantial debt, although exactly how he got there hasn't been particularly well reported. Some suspicions about it being related to automobiles and maybe gambling debt. That doesn't make someone a bad person.

For now, though, even if this ultimately becomes only a small but significant moment in his career, Sharaf gets to be the good guy.

For Gable Steveson, he's the heel. Given his background, he's almost forced into that role.

Sean Sharaf called it out directly during the pre-fight press conference in a bold and rather brilliant move.

  <h3 id="gable-case-title">The 2019 Gable Steveson Investigation</h3>

  <p>
    In June 2019, the Hennepin County Attorney’s Office said it had reviewed
    criminal sexual conduct allegations against University of Minnesota wrestlers
    Gable Steveson and Dylan Martinez.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-1" id="gable-ref-1">1</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    On June 18, the office announced that no charges could be brought at that time
    and that Steveson and Martinez had been released as the 36-hour period during
    which they could be held without charges was set to expire.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-1">1</a></sup>
    Prosecutors said the investigation remained active and that they would continue
    working with Minneapolis police and reviewing additional evidence.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-1">1</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    In its final announcement on December 20, 2019, the Hennepin County Attorney’s
    Office said the Minneapolis Police Department’s Sex Crimes Unit had conducted
    a “thorough and complete investigation” into events that occurred on June 15.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-2" id="gable-ref-2">2</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    The office said the case was reviewed by two veteran prosecutors who specialized
    in sexual-assault cases, as well as senior leadership of the Hennepin County
    Attorney’s Office.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-2">2</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    After that review, the office declined to file charges against Steveson or
    Martinez.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-2">2</a></sup>
    County Attorney Mike Freeman said there were conflicting versions of what
    happened and concluded that there was “inadequate evidence to fairly charge
    and prosecute this case.”<sup><a href="#gable-fn-2">2</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    Freeman also said Minnesota’s laws concerning intoxication and a person’s
    ability to consent restricted how his office could charge the case.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-3" id="gable-ref-3">3</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    At the time, Minnesota law stated that a person who was mentally incapacitated
    or physically helpless could not consent to a sexual act.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-4" id="gable-ref-4">4</a></sup>
    The statute’s definition of “mentally incapacitated” in cases involving alcohol
    or other substances required that the substance had been administered without
    the person’s agreement.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-4">4</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    The same law separately defined a “physically helpless” person to include someone
    who was asleep or unconscious, unable to withhold or withdraw consent because of
    a physical condition, or unable to communicate nonconsent under the circumstances
    described by the statute.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-4">4</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    Minnesota later amended the definition of “mentally incapacitated” in 2021,
    adding circumstances in which a person is under the influence of a substance
    to such a degree that the person is incapable of consenting or of appreciating,
    understanding or controlling their conduct.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-5" id="gable-ref-5">5</a></sup>
    That amendment took effect September 15, 2021.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-5">5</a></sup>
  </p>

  <div class="case-note">
    <strong>Important distinction:</strong>
    Steveson was not criminally charged in the case.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-2">2</a></sup>
    Prosecutors said the accounts of what happened conflicted and that the evidence
    was inadequate to fairly charge and prosecute the case.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-2">2</a></sup>
    They also separately identified Minnesota’s then-existing intoxication-and-consent
    laws as a restriction on how the case could be charged.<sup><a href="#gable-fn-3">3</a></sup>
  </div>

  <div class="case-tldr">
    <strong>TL;DR:</strong>
    Steveson and teammate Dylan Martinez were investigated in 2019 over allegations
    of sexual misconduct. Prosecutors spent months reviewing the case but ultimately
    filed no charges, saying the accounts of what happened conflicted and there was
    not enough evidence to fairly prosecute the case. They also said Minnesota’s law
    at the time limited how cases involving intoxication and consent could be charged.
    Steveson was therefore never convicted or acquitted because the case never went
    to trial.
  </div>

Steveson is legally presumed innocent, but fighting is a promotional business built around conflict, and good versus evil is about as old as promotion itself.

It also doesn't help Steveson's public image that he decided to align himself so closely with Jon Jones.

You'd think a quick temperature check and Google search of Jon Jones would at least make someone reconsider whether that was the best public-facing relationship to build. Jones has spent years surrounded by controversy, failed drug tests, legal trouble, public incidents involving drugs and alcohol, and repeated attempts to present a very different version of himself afterward.

He also strikes me as someone with an immense ego who has always operated largely in his own lane. So when I think about Jon Jones as a coach, I immediately wonder what that actually looks like.

If only I could sit down with Greg Jackson off the record and hear his completely unfiltered thoughts. Or just be a fly on the wall.

Because before thinking about Jon Jones as a coach, it's worth looking at Jon Jones as a fighter who was coached.

  <h3 id="jones-jacksonwink-title">
    Jon Jones Under Jackson-Wink
  </h3>

  <p>
    Jon Jones arrived at Greg Jackson’s Albuquerque camp as a remarkably gifted but
    still developing fighter. He already possessed the traits that would eventually
    define his career: unusual length, solid wrestling foundation, tremendous physical instincts
    and a willingness to attempt techniques that most fighters would never consider.
  </p>

  <p>
    Greg Jackson quickly recognized that Jones was not the kind of athlete who needed
    every exchange mapped out for him. Rather than force him into a rigid system,
    Jackson built structure around Jones’ creativity. He would give him several answers
    to the problems an opponent presented, reinforce the fundamentals underneath them,
    and allow Jones to decide how to put those pieces together during the fight.<sup><a href="#jj-source-1">1</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    That freedom became an important part of Jones’ development. His style was never
    built around one signature weapon. He could wrestle, clinch, kick at range, attack
    with elbows, change stance and improvise from positions that were difficult to
    prepare for. Jackson’s role was less about turning Jones into a particular type of
    fighter and more about giving an unusually versatile athlete a framework in which
    all of those abilities could work together.
  </p>

  <p>
    Mike Winkeljohn became another major part of that development, particularly in
    Jones’ striking and day-to-day training. Over time, Winkeljohn became one of the
    more visible coaches around Jones, while Jackson remained closely associated with
    strategy, opponent study and game planning.<sup><a href="#jj-source-2">2</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    The relationship also showed how much trust Jackson placed in Jones’ own judgment.
    One of the clearest examples came in 2012, when Jones initially leaned toward
    accepting a last-minute fight with Chael Sonnen. Several coaches believed they
    could prepare him in time, but Jackson advised Jones not to let his ego make the
    decision and convinced him to turn the fight down and prepare properly instead.<sup><a href="#jj-source-3">3</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    Jones was not always the perfect training-camp athlete. Before his first fight
    with Alexander Gustafsson, he later admitted that he had only given roughly
    “80 percent” in preparation and entered the fight believing his natural ability
    would carry him through. Gustafsson pushed him harder than anyone previously had,
    and Jones later described the experience as a lesson in how seriously he needed
    to prepare at the championship level.<sup><a href="#jj-source-4">4</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    His independence occasionally extended to his own training choices. During his
    2016 layoff, Jones became heavily involved in powerlifting. After Jones’ fight
    with Ovince Saint Preux, Jackson was caught on a live corner microphone saying
    that the powerlifting had hurt Jones’ conditioning and joking about wanting to
    do an “I-told-you-so dance.” Jones publicly disagreed and said he felt his cardio
    had been fine.<sup><a href="#jj-source-5">5</a></sup>
  </p>

  <p>
    Those moments were exceptions inside a much longer partnership. For most of
    Jones’ championship career, Jackson-Wink provided the structure around one of
    the most adaptable fighters in MMA history. The camp did not eliminate the
    qualities that made Jones unusual; it organized them. Jones was given room to
    experiment, improvise and make decisions for himself while still working inside
    detailed strategic preparation.
  </p>

  <div class="highlight">
    <strong>What made the pairing work:</strong>
    Jackson did not try to make Jon Jones fight like everyone else. He recognized
    early that Jones’ greatest advantage was the sheer number of things he could do,
    and his coaching reflected that. The goal was to give Jones structure without
    removing the unpredictability that made him so difficult to prepare for.
  </div>

  <div class="tldr">
    <strong>TL;DR:</strong>
    Jon Jones developed at Jackson-Wink as an unusually gifted and creative athlete.
    Greg Jackson handled much of the strategy and game planning, while Mike Winkeljohn
    became a major part of Jones’ striking and regular training. Instead of forcing
    Jones into a rigid style, Jackson gave him a framework and allowed him considerable
    freedom to improvise. Jones was not always perfect in camp and sometimes made his
    own training decisions, but that independence was also part of what made him such
    an unusual fighter. Jackson-Wink’s job was less about creating Jon Jones from
    scratch than giving an exceptional athlete the structure to use everything he
    already had.
  </div>

MMA history is full of fighters who come into the sport with a deeply ingrained specialty like wrestling or BJJ and have a difficult time adapting to everything else.

They're usually never going to become great pure strikers. What they can do is develop enough striking to make the rest of their game work: a powerful punch that hurts someone, forces a reaction, or creates the opening for a takedown.

The danger comes when that success convinces them they're better strikers than they actually are. Eventually they will meet someone with good enough takedown defense and clearly superior striking.

Jon Jones was never an elite wrestler in the way Gable Steveson was.

Jones was an elite JUCO wrestler and an excellent national-level high school prospect, but he never proved himself at the highest levels of NCAA Division I wrestling.

Gable is in an entirely different universe as a credentialed wrestler.

He also wasn't even fully committed to MMA when the possibility was first presented to him.

Gable made his MMA debut at 25 years old and his UFC debut at 26.

Jones, meanwhile, wasn't committed to wrestling the way Gable was. He was learning techniques off YouTube so he could fight at 20 years old, then made his UFC debut at 21.

That's an enormous difference in how these two athletes entered MMA.

Jones was a young, raw fighter who eventually ended up in an MMA gym with coaches who recognized his physical attributes, understood what he was capable of, and built around those strengths.

Gable Steveson, at least from what I'm seeing so far, looks like he's being taught to fight like Jon Jones.

By Jon Jones.

And he isn't Jon Jones.

He doesn't have the same physical attributes. He doesn't have Jones' length. He doesn't move like him. More importantly, Steveson does not appear comfortable reacting to feints or punches.

He tends to face everything head-on and rely on his athleticism.

He simply hasn't had enough time to develop. He looks completely ill-prepared to be fighting at the highest level. If the UFC or Jon Jones cared about his development, Gable would be fighting the likes of Sean Sharaf in the regional scene so if he does loses its not with every casual mouth breathing donk as well as hardcore fan watching. 

He needs more time to incubate.

And, in my opinion, he should seriously consider whether Jon Jones should be his primary coach.

If I were building Gable Steveson as an MMA fighter, I would want to build outward from the thing that already makes him extraordinary.

He's a shorter heavyweight with exceptional speed and explosive power. Give him enough of an overhand right that opponents have to respect it, then make them deal with the threat of one of the scariest double legs MMA has ever seen.

Make everything else serve that.

Instead, I'm watching him backpedal, clinch, jab, kick, and do Jon Jones shit that works because Jon Jones is Jon Jones.

Some of those things are uniquely effective because of Jones' body type, instincts, experience, and the years of coaching that went into making him the fighter he became.

You can't just hand that style to someone else.

I picked Gable Steveson to win this fight knowing full well that I could basically see only two outcomes.

Either he would do well enough on the feet to eventually get to his wrestling and take over, or he would get sparked.
